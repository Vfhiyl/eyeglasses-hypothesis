Within national carbon footprints, households are responsible for bulk of the overall direct and indirect 
carbon emissions. Also, the wide spread in household characteristics (e.g. size, income, age) leads not 
only to different carbon footprints, but also includes an underlying difference in the contributions of the 
consumption domains. Therefore, it is essential that policies focussing on climate change mitigation 
should be customized to take into account these differences. In this study we quantify which socio¬ 
economic household characteristics are associated with a higher than average per capita carbon foot¬ 
print and which consumption domains trigger this higher than average carbon footprint. The results are 
quantified for the regional case study of Flanders (Belgium). To obtain a better estimate of direct and 
indirect emissions we draw upon the EXIOBASE2 multi-region input-output dataset combined with the 
regional input-output data and detailed regional household budget survey data. From our analysis, it is 
also clear that the decomposition of the carbon footprint into the relevant consumption domains reveals 
insights that are absent when comparing total carbon footprints. Overall we find that there is a clear link 
between the income distribution and the carbon footprint of consumption, a finding that returns 
frequently in literature for both developed and developing countries. However, this income distribution 
is closely related to several underlying other socio-economic categories (e.g. level of education and 
household size) which do provide further insights useful for policy as they reveal important socio¬ 
economic aspect which needs to be considered.

1. Introduction 

While average national carbon footprints are an indication of 
the required efforts individual countries must undergo in reducing 
carbon emissions, there can be considerable differences in carbon 
footprints depending on socio-economic disparities in society. 
With individual households are responsible for the bulk of overall 
direct and indirect carbon emissions (Baiocchi et al„ 2010; Ivanova 
et al., 2016), policies customized to take into account these 
household differences in carbon footprints are essential. In this 
respect, the increase in inequality and aging of the baby-boom 
generation are two important patterns that not only affect the 
fabric of our society, but are also inextricably linked to changes in 
consumption patterns and the associated carbon footprints. A 
better understanding of the composition of overall carbon foot¬ 
prints is thus fundamental to implement the required policies to

reducing the overall footprint. 

In this light, several studies have looked at the importance of 
household characteristics when disaggregating carbon emissions 
according to the relevant socio-economic constituents (Baiocchi 
et al., 2010; Buchs and Schnepf, 2013; Druckman et al., 2011; 
Druckman and Jackson, 2009; Kerkhof et al., 2009; Reynolds 
et al., 2015; Weber and Matthews, 2008; Wiedenhofer et al., 
2016; Wier et al., 2001). Several of these studies have found sub¬ 
stantial disparities in the carbon footprint associated with con¬ 
sumption between the different types of households. For instance, 
Weber and Matthews (2008), Druckman and Jackson (2009) and 
Wiedenhofer et al. (2016) all reported large differences in carbon 
footprints when comparing the households with the lowest and the 
highest footprint. Kerkhof et al. (2009) found that the environ¬ 
mental impact increased with household expenditure. While the 
environmental impact rose more than proportionally for some 
measures such as smog formation, it increased less than propor¬ 
tionally for other such as climate change. Rather than exploring the 
discrepancies of carbon footprints associated with consumption,

Biichs and Schnepf (2013) analysed the income elasticity of UK 
households relative to several types of emissions. While a rise in 
household income is always associated with increasing emissions, 
transport and indirect emissions react more than proportionately 
to income changes, whereas emissions related to housing are 
generally less elastic. This implies that carbon taxes will affect 
diverse socio-economic groups disparately. Wiedenhofer et al. 
(2016) add to differences in the household carbon footprint due 
to income inequality the difference in carbon footprint between 
urban and rural residents for China. There results show that urban 
residents, 53% of the Chinese population, induce 75% of the national 
household carbon footprint in 2012. 

The literature has thus made significant headway in improving 
our understanding of national carbon footprints. Nevertheless, 
there are still a number of ways in which one can improve upon the 
existing estimates. One important aspect is to make use of more 
detailed and country-specific data to account for the increasing role 
of international trade. Druckman and Jackson (2009) found a rising 
foreign contribution to indirect household carbon emissions over 
time, showing the merit of more accurately accounting for this 
foreign production chain that produces consumption goods. How¬ 
ever, the availability of data at the time restricted the number of 
foreign regions that could be taken up in the analysis. Recently, the 
availability of environmentally extended multi-region input output 
tables (EEMRIO) has greatly expanded the analytic possibilities. In 
this paper, we draw upon the EXIOBASE2 MRIO dataset (Tukker 
et al., 2013; Wood et al., 2015) to obtain a better estimate of 
direct and indirect emissions. Ivanova et al. (2016) also applied the 
EXIOBASE2 dataset to analyse the material-use, land-use, water- 
use and greenhouse gas emissions linked to household expendi¬ 
ture. However, the focus of their paper was on estimating the 
elasticity of expenditure with respect to the average environmental 
impact in a sample that contains a large cross-section of countries. 

Steen-Olsen et al. (2016) are closest in set-up to our paper. They 
combined the Norwegian consumer expenditure survey with 
EXIOBASE2. However, while their focus is on the evolution of the 
carbon footprint and on the relation between different income 
groups and the carbon footprint, we exploit the full detail of the 
Flemish household budget survey to further decompose the carbon 
footprint according to different socio-economic categories. In this 
way, we obtain greater insight into how the carbon footprint varies 
according to different characteristics of households such as 
employment status of the household reference person, the age of 
the household reference person, the size of the household, the 
residential ownership status, etc. This is particularly pertinent for 
policy purposes as varying consumption patterns across different 
socio-economic groups form relevant parameters that need to be 
considered in the formulation of effective and socially acceptable 
climate policy. 

We also perform our analysis on a regional rather than a na¬ 
tional level. This improves the accuracy of our calculations as the 
use of a regional dataset increases the level of disaggregation to 
fully reflect the Flemish economic characteristics. Moreover, using 
more disaggregated data also allows us to control for socio¬ 
demographic differences that may exist within countries and that 
could be particularly relevant for determining the carbon emissions 
associated to consumption (Baiocchi et al., 2010). By linking this 
regional dataset to a global model we also account for global pro¬ 
duction networks. Moreover, the case of a small open economy, 
such as Flanders in which trade is an important economic charac¬ 
teristic, is testament to the added value a detailed regionalised 
MRIO can bring to environmentally related policy analysis. 

The main research question of this paper is stated as follows: 
“Which socio-economic household characteristics are associated with 
a higher than average per capita carbon footprint and which 


consumption domains trigger this higher than average carbon foot¬ 
print?". To analyse this question we compare the average carbon 
footprint of a household according to different household charac¬ 
teristics (size, age, number of children, etc.). Based on the outcome 
we then define those factors that are associated with a higher 
average per person or per household carbon footprint. Next, the 
consumption domains are analysed to understand the underlying 
expenditures resulting in the higher than average carbon footprint. 

The next section describes the methodology and the data that 
we use. Then, we report the results and analyse the differences 
between different socio-economic groups. In the discussion, we 
address the relevance of these results for policy purposes and, 
finally, relevant conclusions are drawn that follow from the 
analysis. 

2. Method and data 

In the following paragraphs we define and explain the concept 
of the carbon footprint, the input-output methodology applied to 
calculate it, the expenditures of households and the different types 
of households. The results encompass the global warming potential 
based on the 1PCC guidelines (IPCC, 2014) and are expressed in ton 
CC> 2 -eq. per household or per person per year, depending on the 
household characteristic under consideration. 

The carbon footprint measures the global greenhouse gas 
emissions emitted in value chains linked to products consumed by 
domestic final demand in one year. The footprint includes direct 
emissions by households, indirect or embodied flows across pro¬ 
duction networks including imports, and excludes exports and 
associated indirect flows of exports. Following the emission ty¬ 
pology of Lebel et al. (2007), these footprints include direct emis¬ 
sions and deemed emissions (i.e. embodied emission of imported 
products), but exclude responsible and logistic emissions (i.e. 
embodied emissions of exported products). The exclusion of ex¬ 
ports (i.e. responsible and logistic emissions) puts the focus on the 
consumption perspective: local emissions related to exported 
products are not taken into account, while deemed emissions are 
included. 

The computation of the carbon footprint is based on input- 
output analysis (IOA). This analysis approximates the emissions 
resulting from final demand expenditures along its entire preced¬ 
ing network of value chains. The general formula that we apply in 
this paper to compute carbon footprints, CF, is based on the Leontief 
inverse and can be represented as (Matthews et al., 2008):

Here, we restrict f from the IO-database to household con¬ 
sumption (so excluding consumption by non-profit institutions, 
governmental consumption, investments and changes in inventories 
and valuables), which is later further specified by consumption do¬ 
mains (COlCOP-classification ). Also, the extension scalar of direct 
household emissions is later specified per consumption domain. To 
calculate the footprint of households in Flanders, this paper applies 
an IOA (Eurostat, 2008). Specifically, we use a regional input-output 
table for Flanders and link this via the import to a globally covered 
multi-region input-output table: if Flanders imports a product, the 
production chain of this product is traced back in the multi-region 
input-output table. In this way, we are able to link the final de¬ 
mand in Flanders to the entire production network preceding it. The 
method applied for the integration of the regional Flemish database 
in a world input-output model is described in Christis et al. (2017). 
Both the regional model for Flanders and the multi-region model are 
used in their full detail without modification. Only the import and 
export of Flanders are linked to the producing or consuming actor in 
the multi-region model. The method is closely related to the multi¬ 
scale multi-region input-output model by Bachmann et al. (2015). 
The difference is that the methodology of Christis et al. adds a sub- 
region to an existing MRIO, while Bachmann et al. replace a nation 
with its sub-regions. However, both methodologies are similar as 
they both account for different sectoral representations. The com¬ 
bination of a local input-output model and a multi-region input- 
output model allows for an analysis based on available local data, 
adapted to the local economic characteristics and including global 
sectoral data. This is particularly relevant for determining the foot¬ 
prints of non-autarchic economy carbon footprints which are 
determined both by local and global specific characteristics. The 
main shortcomings of the IOA for this type of application is the level 
of sector aggregation. Aggregated sectors present the ‘average’ pro¬ 
duction structure which can differ substantially from the production 
structure of a specific product, resulting in under- or overestimations 
of the carbon footprints. Therefore, the results are restricted to 
estimation for the macro-economic products (product groups) and 
provide no details on microlevel products. 

For the methodology described above we use the Flemish EE- 
RIO table (2010 data) which is part of the interregional input- 
output database of Belgium. It contains 124 sectors, nine final de¬ 
mand categories, interregional and international trade, and six 
value added categories. Environmental data in the extension tables 
comprise the emissions data necessary to calculate the carbon 
footprint. In the input-output model of Flanders, consumption is 
defined according to the residential (national) concept i.e. house¬ 
hold expenditure of all residents in Flanders and abroad. The input- 
output tables follow the ESA95 rules. 

We then link this Flemish IO table via the trade link to EXIO- 
BASE2 (2007 data; industry-by-industry tables). EXIOBASE2 is a 
global EE-MRIO database produced in the context of the project 
‘Compiling and Refining of Economic and Environmental Accounts’ 
(CREEA). The database covers 200 products and 163 industries for 
43 countries and 5 rest-of-world regions. The extension tables 
include 15 land use types, employment broken down into three 
skill levels, 48 types of raw materials and 172 types of water use. 
Flanders is indirectly included in the EXIOBASE2 database via 
Belgium (Tukker et al., 2013; Wood et al„ 2014).

This study compiles a final demand matrix for households in 
Flanders for each household characteristic. This matrix describes 
for a given household characteristic the expenditures of households 
at a detailed level based on the COICOP-classification. These 
matrices are compiled from the original final demand vector 
available in the input-output model, but reconstructed for each 
household characteristic using the household budget survey. The 
datasets and procedure are explained below. 

The basis for the expansion of the final demand vector of Flemish 
households is the household final consumption expenditure ac¬ 
count, which is part of the national accounts and covers expendi¬ 
tures incurred by households to acquire consumption goods and 
services. It excludes expenditures devoted by households to the 
acquisition of dwellings, which constitute an investment in fixed 
assets, and those devoted to the acquisition of valuables' (Eurostat, 
2008). The accounts use the COICOP-classification: a classification of 
individual consumption expenditures incurred by households ac¬ 
cording to their purpose. The household consumption expenditures 
are classified in 12 domains (level 1), which can be further dis¬ 
aggregated into more detailed COICOP levels 2 and 3. 

The household budget surveys (HBS; see Eurostat (2003)) are 
the main source behind the compilation of the household final 
consumption expenditure in national accounts. HBS for Belgium are 
available both at a national level and at the level of the three re¬ 
gions (Flanders, Wallonia and Brussels). The surveys mainly focus 
on consumption expenditure and describe the expenditure struc¬ 
ture of different types of households (i.e. household characteristics) 
at a detailed COICOP-level. For the case of Flanders we use the HBS 
provided by Statistics Belgium that contains consumption data for 
different household characteristics. Specifically, the HBS reports 
average expenditure per household and per year by: 

- the income groups using deciles; 

- the number of elderly (>65) per household (household with no 
elderly, households with only elderly and mixed households); 

- the number of children (<16) per household (households with 
children and households without children); 

- the number of active people per household (households with 0, 
1 or 2 and more active people); 

- the statute owner or renter; 

- the age of the reference person (households with a reference 
person younger than 30 years old, between 30 and 39, 40—49, 
50—59 or older than 60 years); 

- type of household (single, 2 adults, 3 or more adults, single with 
dependent child(ren), 2 adults with dependent child(ren) or 3 or 
more adults with dependent child(ren)); 

- the size of the household (single, 2,3,4, 5 or 6 or more persons); 

- the employee statute of the reference person (self-employed, 
employee, unemployed, pensioner or other non-active); and 

- the age of the oldest person in the household (the oldest person 
is younger than 65 years, between 65 and 74 years or above 75 
years old).

gories by defining a reference person. Here, the reference person is 
defined as the person contributing the most to the financial income 
of the household. In the case where two people have equal

However, the data from the household budget survey is not 
directly transferable to the household expenditures in the input- 
output tables format. Data in household budget surveys is 
defined in purchasers prices inclusive non-deductible VAT. While, 
the 10-tables are expressed in basic prices (separating taxes and 
subsidies), and as such distinguish between the trade margins and 
product value for the products purchased by households. It means 
that trade margins are allocated to the trade sectors and the 
product value is allocated to the sector (or sectors in case of mul¬ 
tiple products) producing the product. In order to match the two, 
allocation tables are available. These tables allocate the final de¬ 
mand vector of households in different consumption categories e.g. 
food, housing, and as such links the consumption categories (COI- 
COP-classification) and the purchases from sectors (NACE-classifi- 
cation), both domestic purchases and imports. Allocation tables 
link the trade margins to the value of the product and combine the 
purchases of products of different sectors into one COlCOP-category 
(e.g. bread and cereals (01.1.1) is a combination of products of 
different sectors). The allocation of product (or product groups) to 
consumption domains is based on the definitions of the COICOP- 
domains as described by the UNSD (UNSD, 2003). 

An important drawback of the applied methodology is that the 
IOA provides only uses one carbon footprint intensity per product 
group. Although households can consume different products 
within the same product group, the IOA allows not to reflect this 
different in consumption pattern within one product group into the 
carbon footprints. For example, higher income households may 
consume higher quality meat product compared to lower income 
households. However, as the model only provides one (average) 
carbon footprint intensity per euro of meat products, this difference 
in consumption pattern is not translated into a difference in carbon 
footprint. The increasing quality orientation of households with 
rising income is neglected using our methodology (Girod and De 
Haan, 2010). This results in an overestimation of the carbon foot¬ 
print for the households systematically choosing for higher quality 
products, but also underestimates the impact of households sys¬ 
tematically choosing for cheaper low quality products. 

Allocation tables for Flanders are developed (via a bottom-up 
approach) by the Federal Planning Bureau and are structured in 
different consumption categories (40 according to COICOP 3-digit 
division for 2010) and assigned to industries that are identified by 
the NACE-classification conform the IO-tables. In a next step, the 
consumption categories are extended to 59 categories according to 
COICOP 3-digit classification, based on the allocation tables for 2003 
that distinguish 387 COICOP categories. The allocation tables for 
2003 were also developed by the Federal Planning Bureau, based on 
the Household Budget Enquiry (HBE) for Belgium (Bilsen et al., 2010). 

The attribution of direct emissions from households (e HH direct 
from the carbon footprint equation) to the COICOP-consumption 
domains of housing (fuel use for heating and cocking) and trans¬ 
port (fuel use for road transport). The attribution is based on a 
bottom up analysis, using regional energy statistics (energy bal¬ 
ance). These direct emission coefficients (defined per euro expen¬ 
diture of the corresponding consumption domain) are linked to 
household categories based on expenditures. 

In conclusion, the results in the manuscripts are based on input- 
output analysis using a input-output model compiled from: 

- household expenditure data from the household budget survey 
data for the year 2010; 

- a regional input-output model for Flanders of data year 2010; 

- a multiregional input-output model Exiobase v.2 for data year 
2007. 


3. Results 

The results are structured according to the different household 
characteristics that are available in HBS and presented in Fig. 1. The 
average household in Flanders consists of 2.378 persons including 
on average 0.446 children (<18y). 39% of the households have 
children, on average 1.742. The yearly expenditures of the average 
household sum up to 36,737.22 EUR and our analysis reveals that 
average household expenditure is linked to a carbon footprint of 
33.1 ton C02-eq/yr. Based on estimates in the literature, we can 
make a direct comparison with other European countries. UK 
households have been found to emit on average 20.18 tons CC> 2 -eq./ 
yr over the period 2006—2009 (Biichs and Schnepf, 2013) and 21.5 
tons C02-eq./yr in 2004 (Druckman and Jackson, 2009). Norwegian 
households emitted 22.3 tons C02-eq./yr per average household in 
2012 (Steen-Olsen et al., 2016). Of these studies, only the latter 
applies EXIOBASE2. The reported numbers of Biichs and Schnepf 
(2013) and Druckman and Jackson (2009) likely differ strongly 
from our estimates for Flemish households as we apply the EXIO- 
BASE2 MRIO while they use different and less extensive I/O models 
to calculate the total average C02-eq./yr embodied by consumption. 
Indeed, using the EXIOBASE2 dataset, Ivanova et al. (2016) found 
that the average per capita footprint of UK citizens is higher than 
that of Belgian citizens possibly indicating that the UK household 
carbon footprints are lower bound estimates of the actual carbon 
footprint. In contrast, Wiedenhofer et al. (2016) show that Chinese 
households only have an average per capita carbon footprint of 1.7 
tons CC> 2 -eq./yr in 2012 (however, very rich urban dwellers have a 
carbon footprint of 6.4 tons C02-eq.). 

Fig. 2 panel (a) reveals that the three COICOP-categories with 
the highest carbon footprint are housing, water, electricity, gas and 
other fuels (04; 36%), transport (07; 21%) and food and non¬ 
alcoholic beverages (01; 16%). Although the order of the cate¬ 
gories contributing most varies, the importance of these three 
categories in the average household carbon footprint has been re¬ 
ported elsewhere in the literature as well (Steen-Olsen et al., 2016). 

In what follows we will report on each of the household char¬ 
acteristics individually and discuss our general findings. Although 
we also performed the carbon footprint calculations based on the 
number of children in a household and the age of the oldest person 
of the household, these will not be further discussed in the text as 
they overlap with some of the other household subdivisions that 
are already included in our discussion.

footprint between the lowest and highest income category. 


3.2. Employee status of the reference person 

While there has been considerable focus in the literature on the 
disparate consumption patterns of different income classes and 
their associated carbon footprints (e.g. Sommer and Kratena (2017); 
Birchs and Schnepf (2013); Girod and De Haan (2010)), much less 
has been reported on carbon footprint differences for other socio¬ 
economic indicators. However, from a policy perspective these 
are equally interesting as income deciles are made up of hetero¬ 
geneous population groups. One other socio-economic factor 
influencing consumption-based carbon footprints is the employee 
status of the household reference person, to which we turn our 
attention now (Fig. 2, panel (c)). 

The total carbon footprint per person of a household for the 
different employee statuses of the reference person is comparable 
across the household categories, except when the reference person 
of the household is retired as they have a higher per person carbon 
footprint of 16.6 tons CC> 2 -eq./yr per capita. On the other side of the 
spectrum, the self-employed category shows the lowest per person 
carbon footprint of 12.2 ton C02-eq./yr per capita. The other three 
categories are comparable to the self-employed category with 12.5 
ton C02-eq./yr per person in the other non-active category, 12.9 ton 
C0 2 -eq./yr in the employed person category and 13.0 ton C02-eq./yr 
in the unemployed person category. The expenditures per person 
are comparable across the categories, varying between 12.581 EUR 
to 14.559 EUR per person per year, except for the pensioner cate¬ 
gory having an average expenditure of 18.107 EUR per person per 
year. The size of the average household, varying between 1.6 and 
1.7, is comparable between the categories unemployed, pensioner 
and other non-active, but is higher for the employee (2.7) and self-

spent indoors by pensioners. 


3.3. Type of the household 

When we divide the households into types according to the 
number of adults and children, one would anticipate considerable 
discrepancies in carbon footprints between the different types. This 
is indeed what we observe (Fig. 2, panel (d)). One clear takeaway 
from our results is that the carbon footprint rises with the number 
of adults in the household. Also, households with children have a 
higher carbon footprint compared to the same household without 
children. Given these facts, households with one single adult and 
no children have the lowest carbon footprint of 20.9 ton C02-eq./yr 
and households with 3 or more adults and children are at the other 
end of the spectrum with a carbon footprint of 42.9 ton C02-eq./yr. 
Adding children to the average households results in a higher car¬ 
bon footprint: on average + 9592 ton C02-eq./yr per household 
or +5506 ton C02-eq./yr per child. However, it is clear that the 
increase in the carbon footprint is not proportional to the number 
of persons. It is thus interesting to note that while transport (07) 
and housing, water, electricity, gas and other fuels (04) are the 
predominant consumption factors driving the increase in the car¬ 
bon footprint across household types, the latter factor constitutes a 
much larger fraction of the overall carbon footprint of single adults 
compared to households with three adults or more with children. 

3.4. Size of the household 

Fig. 2 panel (e) takes a closer look at the effects of the size of a 
household on the carbon footprint. Note that this part shows results 
per capita rather than per household as was the case for the type of 
the household. One can see that while an increase in the size of the 
household can be linked to an increasing carbon footprint, the 
average carbon footprint per person decreases with an increasing 
household size. A household consisting out of a single adult 
without children has an average carbon footprint of 20.9 ton CO 2 - 
eq./yr, while this is only 6.8 ton C02-eq./yr per person in a house¬ 
hold of six or more persons. The per person carbon footprint de¬ 
creases with 23% from a single to a household composed of two 
people, with 21% from a two to three persons household, with 16% 
from a three to four persons household and with 14% from a four to 
five persons household. These results suggest that there are certain 
‘economies of scale’ and advantages of sharing that eventually 
result in a lower per person carbon footprint when household size 
increases. The two consumption domains that are mainly driving 
this result again are housing, water, electricity, gas and other fuels 
(04) and transport (07). While the carbon footprint resulting from 
food and non-alcoholic beverages (01), clothing and footwear (03) 
and recreation and culture (09), hotels and restaurant (11) also 
show minor signs of decreasing, they are overall fairly independent 
of the household size.

4. Discussion 

Thus far we have mostly focused on the observed trends in the 
different socio-economic categories. However, several of these 
findings are relevant from a policy perspective targeting climate 
change mitigation. Indeed, Baiocchi et al. (2010) finds that around 
74 percent of total climate emissions in the UK can be linked to UK 
households, making private households a particularly potent target 
group in the fight against climate change. For comparison, this is 68 
percent in Flanders. 

One important conclusion from our results is that climate pol¬ 
icies also have an important socio-economic aspect which needs to 
be considered, as also found by Btichs and Schnepf (2013) and 
discussed by Gough (2013). Housing, water, electricity, gas and 
other fuels make up the largest component of the household 
footprint irrespective of the income decile in which the household 
is situated. Nevertheless, aggressive policies that penalize house¬ 
holds for the carbon footprint associated to this consumption 
category risk aggravating existing socio-economic inequality. 
Housing and related utilities makes up the bulk of the household 
carbon budget of low income households, but a much smaller 
fraction for high-income households. Hence, for policy to be so¬ 
cially tenable and ultimately effective a clear understanding of such 
sensitivities is required prior to the establishment of policy. Our 
results indicate that the GHG emission intensity per monetary unit 
of expenditure decreases with rising income (see also Kerkhof et al. 
(2009)), making pure carbon taxation based on the carbon foot¬ 
print of households regressive in nature. 

We also find that the per person carbon footprint decreases with 
rising household size as consumption domains like housing and 
related utilities and transport are distributed over an increasing 
number of household members, thereby lowering the per person 
impact. However, the ‘scale economies’ and benefits of sharing seem 
to be mostly limited to these consumption domains. The promotion 
of a sharing economy could be particularly helpful in combatting 
household related carbon emissions with respect to transport. 
Overall food consumption, the carbon footprint of which declines 
with the size of the household, seems to be mostly insensitive to the 
number of household members. Addressing the carbon footprint of 
this category will thus require a different approach. More specif¬ 
ically, it is particularly interesting that the carbon footprint of food 
consumption is strongly driven by meat consumption and con¬ 
sumption of animal products (e.g. milk). We find that the con¬ 
sumption of meat is an important factor in determining carbon 
footprint differences related to food consumption between income 
deciles and age groups. Yet, caution is also warranted in interpreting 
this result. Girod and De Haan (2010) show that household expen¬ 
diture data also cover up all quality differences within a certain 
consumption domain. Particularly, higher income households and 
older households might consume higher priced meats, so that the 
average GHG emission intensity of meat consumption hides a lower 
intensity for higher income groups, thereby overestimating the 
carbon footprint for high income households and underestimating 
the impact for low impact households. 

From our analysis, it is also clear that the decomposition of the 
carbon footprint into the relevant consumption domains reveals 
insights that are absent when comparing total carbon footprints. 
For instance, renters and dwelling owners have similar average per

Overall we find that there is a clear link between the income 
distribution and the carbon footprint of consumption, a finding that 
returns frequently for both developed (e.g. Steen-Olsen et al. (2016) 
and Sommer and Kratena (2017)) and developing countries (e.g. 
Han et al. (2015)) alike. However, the income distribution is closely 
related to several underlying other socio-economic categories. 
Richer households will most likely also be older, while older 
households are in turn increasingly more likely to consist of fewer 
individuals and thus have higher per person footprints. While we 
have not addressed these clusters of population groups, these are 
also worth considering when formulating policy. 

5. Conclusion 

This paper analyses the carbon footprint of the consumption of 
Flanders (Belgium) taking into account different household char¬ 
acteristics. It uses the EXIOBASE2 MRIO which has been extended 
to include this region particularly and integrates the household 
budget surveys that were carried out at the Flemish regional level. 
By applying the analysis at the regional rather than the national 
level we are able to obtain a detailed account of the carbon foot¬ 
print of a more homogenous socio-demographic group of con¬ 
sumers. Moreover, applying the EXIOBASE2 dataset to a small open 
economy of Flanders exploits the full power of this dataset in 
obtaining better estimates for the indirect footprint related to 
consumption. 

Overall, we find that the Flemish households have an average 
carbon footprint of 33.1 ton CO 2 eq./yr per household or 14.0 ton 
C0 2 eq./yr per person. This carbon footprint increases mono- 
tonically with the income decile in which the family is situated. 
However, different consumption categories contribute differently 
to this increase to the carbon footprint. While housing, water, 
electricity, gas and other fuels generally make up more than half of 
the total carbon footprint of households in the lowest income 
decile, it constitutes less than a third of the total average carbon 
footprint of top income decile households. The low average 
household size and apparent larger heating requirements cause 
households where the main income comes from a pension to have a 
carbon footprint that is around 30 percent higher per person than 
other households. We also find that the carbon footprint does not 
rise proportionately to the number of people that are present in the 
household. When one looks at the per person carbon footprint of 
larger households, there are certain ‘economies of scale’ and ad¬ 
vantages of sharing household consumption, particularly with 
respect to housing and related utilities. Remarkably, while the 
actual composition of the carbon footprint of consumption differs 
between renters and statute owners of residences, their carbon 
footprint is similar on average. 

Policies generally attempt to directly limit the carbon emissions 
that result from consumption through several types of in¬ 
terventions. However, this creates shifts in consumption patterns 
that will offset (part of) the carbon emissions which the policy 
attempted to reduce. Given its importance in the efficacy of miti¬ 
gating the climate impact of consumption, we intend to look closer 
into this rebound effect in future research (Bjelle et al., 2018; 
Chitnis et al., 2014; Druckman et al., 2011). It is clear that even 
within a more homogenous socio-demographic region such as 
Flanders there are still large socio-economic differences within the 
population that need to be considered in policy design. Moreover, 
rebound effects are likely to differ strongly between these groups 
and across different consumption domains, making it a particularly 
important topic for gaining further insights.
